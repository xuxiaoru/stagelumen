Most rental houses start by buying variety and end up owning a warehouse of fixtures that cannot be sent out together. The inventory that makes money is not the most impressive one; it is the one where any two units can be swapped into the same rig without a repatch, and where a single failure does not cancel the job.

## The short answer

Buy two fixture types in quantity rather than five types in pairs: a small beam and a zoom wash, eight to twelve units in total. At EXW prices from US$96 to US$159 each, a ten-fixture starter fleet costs roughly US$1,100 to US$1,500 before cases and cabling, and it covers small club, bar, church and corporate work — the majority of a young rental house's booked jobs.

## What fixture types cover most rental requests?

Four requests account for almost everything a small rental house is asked for, and two fixture types cover all four.

| The client asks for | What actually delivers it | Fixture type |
|---|---|---|
| Aerial beams, club punch | Narrow beam, high output per degree | Beam or mini beam |
| Colour wash over a stage | Wide, even field with zoom | Zoom wash |
| Front light on a performer | Narrow, even, gobo-capable | Spot (a wash with zoom often covers it) |
| Effects and movement | Position changes, colour changes | Either, if quantity is available |

The remaining requests — pixel-mapped walls, kinetic effects, lasers — need a different fixture family and a different programming skill. They are second-phase purchases, not starter stock.

## How should the fleet be standardised?

Standardisation is what turns a pile of fixtures into an asset:

- **One channel footprint** across the set, so a patch works whichever unit goes out.
- **One clamp type and one safety bond**, so prep is uniform.
- **One case size**, so the van load never needs re-planning.
- **One spares bin** — fuses, a spare fan, a spare set of gaskets and a spare power cable per fixture type.
- **One firmware version**, recorded, so a returned fixture is not subtly different from the rest.

A fleet where every unit is interchangeable can be sub-rented out whole and absorbed back without a patch change. A fleet of mixed models cannot.

## Should the fleet be bought new or used?

New, for a first fleet, for three reasons: a warranty that covers the first season's failures, consistent firmware and colour across the set, and a supplier relationship you will need for spares. Used fixtures make sense later, when you already have a spares bin and in-house repair capability and know exactly which models your market rents.

The exception is a single hero fixture for a specific client's recurring show. That is a business decision about one contract, not an inventory decision about a fleet.

## When should the inventory grow?

Add a third fixture type when a client asks for it by name and you have declined the job twice. Add quantity when you are turning work away or sub-renting in. Both signals are better than a specification upgrade, which changes the capability of one unit rather than the capacity of the fleet.

## How should the fleet be cased and stored?

Cases decide whether a fixture is still rentable after twenty jobs. Three rules cover most of it.

- **One case per fixture, or one case per matched pair**, sized so the clamp and safety bond travel with the fixture. A clamp that travels separately will eventually travel with the wrong fixture, or not return at all.
- **Label the case outside with the model and an asset number**, and the same number on the fixture. A fleet where the case number and fixture number disagree is a fleet that loses track of warranty dates.
- **Store dry and off the floor.** Moisture is what kills fixtures between jobs, and a garage floor in a humid month does more damage than a season of use.

Keep one spare case for every ten fixtures. Cases fail at the hinges and the wheels, and a fleet that cannot case a fixture cannot rent it.
