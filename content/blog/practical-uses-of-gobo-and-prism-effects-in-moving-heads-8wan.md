Gobos and prisms are the two effects that turn a moving head from a coloured light into something that draws on the room. They are also the two most often bought speculatively and then never used, because nobody worked out what job they were meant to do.

## The short answer

A gobo puts a shape in the beam — a pattern, a logo, a break-up that makes a flat wash look like light through trees. A prism multiplies the beam into a cluster. Buy gobos when you need an image or texture on a surface, and prisms when you need to fill air. If the pattern needs to sit in one place for months, use a dedicated projector instead of a moving head: it is brighter per dollar and it takes your own artwork.

## What does a gobo actually do?

A gobo is a stencil in the optical path. Metal gobos are cut sheet and give hard-edged geometric patterns; glass gobos are printed or etched and carry photographs, logos and gradients. Placed in the beam, the fixture projects that image at whatever it is pointed at.

Two things decide whether the result looks professional: the quality of the gobo and the evenness of the beam behind it. A glass gobo in a fixture with a hot centre will show the hot centre, which is why gobo work favours profile and spot fixtures over washes.

| Gobo type | What it projects | Best for | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Metal, fixed | Geometric patterns, hard edges | Break-ups, texture on a wall | No photographic detail |
| Glass, fixed | Logos, photographs, gradients | Branding, architectural projection | Costs more, can fade with heat |
| Glass, rotating | The same, spinning | Movement in a projected image | Needs a rotating wheel |
| Indexing / scrolling | Several gobos selectable | One fixture doing several looks | More channels, more cost |

## When should you use a dedicated gobo projector instead of a moving head?

When the image does not move. A logo on a lobby wall, a company name on a show floor, a pattern on a building facade — these are installations, and a moving head spends money on motors and a yoke that will never turn.

## What is a prism for?

A prism splits one beam into several. A three-facet prism gives three beams in a ring, an eight-facet gives eight, and a linear prism fans them into a line. Rotating the prism makes the cluster sweep, which is the effect that fills a large room from a handful of fixtures.

| Prism type | What the audience sees | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| 3-facet circular | Three beams rotating around a centre | Slow sweeps, elegant rather than busy |
| 8-facet circular | Eight beams in a ring | Big aerial fan effects at speed |
| Linear (6-row) | A fan or curtain of beams | Walls of light across an audience |
| Prism + gobo | Patterned copies of the same image | Texture multiplied across a surface |

Prisms need air. Like beams, they are an aerial effect: with no haze and a low ceiling, an eight-facet prism produces eight bright dots on the back wall and nothing else.

## What should you not do with them?

Do not buy a gobo wheel for a rig that will only ever wash. Do not put a prism on a fixture hung at 3 m. And do not order a metal gobo when the artwork has a gradient or a photograph in it — it will not reproduce, and the money is wasted on a stencil that cannot carry the image.
